8.1 Setup and hold

Teaches Setup and hold (primitive). About 5 minutes.

Since level 2, a flip-flop has taken D at the rising edge. On the chip, D must hold still for a little more than an instant: from a short time before the edge, the setup time, until a short time after it, the hold time. A change inside that window may leave the flip-flop with the old value, the new one, or neither for a while; level 9 looks at that.

A path, from edge to edge

A path runs from one flip-flop to the next; in this level both are on the same clock. It starts at a rising edge: the first flip-flop's Q changes a short time after it, its clock-to-output time. The change then passes through the logic and the wires, and must reach the second flip-flop's D at least a setup time before the next rising edge.

Figure 1 is a model of one path: two of level 2's flip-flops on a 10 ns clock, with an inverter between them, a NOT gate (~, lesson 1.2), that takes 6 ns, a delay written into the testbench.

The model leaves out what the chip adds: its flip-flops change at the very edge and need no setup time. On the chip each takes a fraction of a nanosecond, and the logic and the wires take what Vivado's timing report says they take (lesson 8.3).

A path that takes longer than the time between two edges arrives after the edge that should have taken it. In a simulation like this one, the next flip-flop then takes the new value an edge late. On the chip, a change inside the window can do worse.

Hold, the other side of the edge

A path can also be too quick. The edge that starts it is an edge for the second flip-flop too, which is still taking its old value: a new value that arrives within the hold time after that edge can spoil it. On one clock this rarely happens, because even a path with no logic takes the first flip-flop's clock-to-output time and a wire. Vivado checks hold as well as setup on every path, and the paths that come closest are the shortest.

Questions

Question 1 of 3. Predict q3

Lesson 8.1's model of a path, with a slower one beside it: an inverter that takes 12 ns, from q1 to d3, on a 10 ns clock. Another of level 2's flip-flops, ff3, takes d3 at each rising edge. Cycles 0 and 1 are drawn for you. Set q3 for the rest.

Question 2 of 3. Put the steps in order

ff1 and ff2 are two flip-flops on one clock, with logic between ff1's Q and ff2's D. Put in order what happens on this path when it meets the setup time.

  • A rising edge reaches ff1.
  • The change passes through the logic and the wires.
  • It reaches ff2's D, at least a setup time before the next edge.
  • A clock-to-output time later, ff1's Q changes.
  • At the next rising edge, ff2 takes the new value.
Show answer and explanation

In order:

  1. A rising edge reaches ff1.
  2. A clock-to-output time later, ff1's Q changes.
  3. The change passes through the logic and the wires.
  4. It reaches ff2's D, at least a setup time before the next edge.
  5. At the next rising edge, ff2 takes the new value.

A path starts at an edge, and ff1's Q changes a short time after it, never at the same instant. The new value then takes as long as the logic and the wires take, and it must be at ff2's D a setup time before the next edge for ff2 to take it there. The tempting slip is to put the setup time after the edge: setup comes before the edge, hold after it.

The report says 0.

Read Logic Levels: 0. The path runs from x_reg[6][8] straight into x_reg[7][8], one register of the chain into the next, with only a wire between: 0.197 ns in all. The shortest paths come closest to breaking hold, because their new value arrives soonest after the edge. This one still arrives 0.121 ns later than hold needs: the Slack line at the top. Path Type names the check: Hold, worked out for the chip at its fastest (Min at Fast Process Corner), where data arrives soonest.
