10.4 Debugging on the board

Teaches Integrated logic analyser (design). About 6 minutes.

A testbench tests what you thought of. When a design passes its testbench and still does the wrong thing on the board, the cause is often something the testbench never did: a button that bounces longer, a computer a little faster, a button that reads 1 when pressed where the testbench drove it the other way (lesson 7.4). Then you need to see what the chip itself does.

A logic analyser inside the FPGA

An integrated logic analyser, AMD's ILA, is a circuit Vivado builds into your design from the FPGA's own logic and block RAM. At every rising edge of a clock you choose, it records the nets you chose, its probes, in its memory. When a trigger condition you set comes true, say the FIFO's count reaches 4, it keeps that moment and the samples around it (where the trigger sits in the window is a setting), and sends them over the USB cable to Vivado's Hardware Manager, which draws them as a waveform, as a simulator does.

Adding one

First mark the nets to watch, so that synthesis keeps them and their names: with the attribute (* mark_debug = "true" *) on their declarations, or with a constraint, as Listing 1 does for echo_ila, the course's build of this level's project with an ILA.

After synthesis, Set Up Debug makes an ILA, connects the marked nets to its probes, and writes the commands into your constraints. Listing 2 is the course's build giving the same commands for echo_ila: a clock, a depth of 1024 samples, and three probes. Implementation, Vivado's steps after synthesis (tidying the netlist, then place and route), builds the ILA with the rest of the design. Then program the board with the bitstream and the probes file, .ltx, which tells Vivado which net each probe is, set a trigger, and run it.

What it costs

An ILA is hardware: block RAM for its samples, and LUTs and flip-flops for its probes and its trigger. Its sample depth is how many samples its memory holds, 1024 here: at 100 MHz, 10.24 µs of the design's life. And each new choice of probes means implementing again.

You want to watch three nets of a design with an ILA as it runs on the board. Put the steps in order.

  • Put mark_debug on the nets you want to see, in the HDL.
  • Program the board with the bitstream and the probes file, then run the trigger.
  • Implement the design and write the bitstream.
  • Synthesise the design.

In order:

  1. Put mark_debug on the nets you want to see, in the HDL.
  2. Synthesise the design.
  3. Run Set Up Debug: choose the ILA's clock, its sample depth and its probes.
  4. Implement the design and write the bitstream.
  5. Program the board with the bitstream and the probes file, then run the trigger.

The marks go in first, so that synthesis keeps the nets and their names. Set Up Debug works on the synthesised design: it makes the ILA from the marked nets and writes the commands into the constraints. Implementation then builds the ILA into the chip, and only a programmed board, with the probes file that names each probe, can be triggered.

How many RAMB18 blocks does echo_ila use?

Show answer and explanation

The report says 1.

One RAMB18, half a block RAM tile, which is why the tile count reads 0.5. It holds the samples: 1024 samples of 12 bits are 12,288 bits, which fit in its 18 kilobits. A deeper ILA, or more probe bits, needs more of them.

Question 3 of 3. Choose the answer

You want an ILA to record one whole character on the board's rx line, from its start bit to its stop bit. At 115,200 baud a character lasts about 86.8 µs. With the trigger at the first sample of the window, which capture holds all of it?

  • 16384 samples at 100 MHz, triggered on rx falling.
  • 1024 samples at 100 MHz, triggered on rx falling.
  • 4096 samples at 100 MHz, triggered on rx falling.
  • 1024 samples on a 200 MHz clock, triggered on rx falling.
Show answer and explanation

The answer: 16384 samples at 100 MHz, triggered on rx falling.

A capture lasts its sample depth times the clock period. 1024 samples of 10 ns are 10.24 µs and 4096 are 40.96 µs: both end long before the stop bit. A faster clock makes the window shorter, 5.12 µs at 200 MHz. 16384 samples last 163.84 µs, enough. Each sample costs block RAM, and the ILA stores one at every edge whatever its probes do. So it is often cheaper to watch uart_rx's data and valid and trigger on valid: the whole byte is on data in that one cycle, and 1024 samples show it.
