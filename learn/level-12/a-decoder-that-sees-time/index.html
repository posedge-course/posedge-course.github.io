12.1 A decoder that sees time

Teaches Four-bit weights (circuit). About 7 minutes.

Level 11's decoder assumed every check was measured correctly. Real measurements go wrong too, and a single wrong one looks like an error. So this level's decoder reads several rounds of checks at once, a grid in space and time, and looks at where a check changed between rounds.

The network

At code distance 5 with five rounds, the grid is 6 steps of time, each 4 × 4 cells: 96 voxels, positions in a volume. A cell holds up to 8 of the code's sites, 4 qubits and 4 checks, so this grid has fewer, larger positions than level 11's 9 × 9. Each voxel has 12 bits: 8 say which of its cell's sites exist, and 4 which of its checks changed. Three hidden layers with 3 × 3 × 3 kernels make 128 channels each. A skip connection takes the 12 input channels past them to the last layer, which turns 128 + 12 = 140 channels into 12 outputs: an X and a Z correction (lesson 11.1) for each of 4 qubits, and for each of 4 checks, whether its measurement was wrong (Figure 1).

Four-bit weights

The network has 971,568 weights. At 8 bits each they would need 7,772,544 bits, 156% of the chip's block RAM. The two middle layers hold 442,368 weights each, so they keep only 4 bits a weight, and the first and last layers 8 (16 for the skip): about 4.26 million bits, 85.7% of the block RAM. This mixed precision makes the decoder fit, and the middle layers are where it costs least: cut to 4 bits on its own, layer two kept the decisions close to those of the network with full-width weights; the small last layer, cut on its own, did not.

Signed and unsigned

A hidden layer's outputs, its activations, come after ReLU, so they are never negative: unsigned 8-bit numbers, 0 to 255. Weights are signed. One lane of a hidden layer's engine, the hardware that works the layer out, multiplies eight activations by their weights, adds the products, and adds those to a 32-bit sum that started from the bias.

In SystemVerilog, one unsigned operand makes the whole expression unsigned: a weight of −1, 1111, would count as 15. So mac4 (Listing 1), a lane in small with one product a cycle, puts act into sact, declared signed, with a 0 on top so that 128 to 255 stay positive.

Questions

Question 1 of 3. Predict pos

Lesson 12.1's mac4 with the bias −3. At each rising edge where valid is 1, acc takes act times w, added to the bias when first is 1 and to the sum so far when it is 0. pos is 1 while acc is more than 0. Cycles 0 and 1 are drawn for you. Set pos for the rest.

Question 2 of 3. Predict pos

Lesson 12.1's mac4 with the bias 5 and some negative weights. At each rising edge where valid is 1, acc takes act times w, added to the bias when first is 1 and to the sum so far when it is 0. pos is 1 while acc is more than 0. w is drawn in hexadecimal: E is 1110, which as a signed 4-bit number is −2. In cycle 0, pos is still 1 from the sum before. Set pos for every cycle.

Question 3 of 3. Find the bug

The bug is on line 12, input logic [3:0] w, // -8 to 7. It should read input logic signed [3:0] w,.

The weight port is not declared signed. One unsigned operand makes the whole product unsigned, so 1110 counts as 14: 2 × 14 = 28, and 5 + 28 = 33. Declared signed, it is −2, and the sum is 5 − 4 = 1. The two agree for the weights 0 to 7, which is why the first sums pass. Writing $signed(w) in the product would also work, but the weight is −8 to 7 everywhere, so its port is where its type belongs.
