Clock-crossing patterns

A signal from another clock, or from a button, can change inside a flip-flop's window around the edge, and the flip-flop can hang between 0 and 1 for a while (lesson 9.1). No simulation shows it and no timing report checks it, so every crossing goes through one of these patterns.

A level that changes now and then, such as a buttonA synchroniserOne signal only, never the bits of a bus; it arrives two or three edges later
A pulseA toggle, a synchroniser, and a register that turns each change of the level, up or down, into a pulsePulses at least two cycles of the reading clock apart
A value, now and thenA handshakeThe value holds still until the other side answers
A value every cycleAn asynchronous FIFOIts counts cross in Gray code, with their bus skew constrained
A resetA reset synchroniserIt starts at once and ends at a clock edge

A level: the synchroniser

  • Two flip-flops in a row on the clock that reads. The first may hang; the second takes its output a whole cycle later, by which time it has almost always settled. Nothing else reads the first.
  • ASYNC_REG marks the pair: Vivado places them close together, and its CDC report knows them.
  • A change arrives two edges later, or three. Two signals that change together can arrive an edge apart, so the bits of a bus cannot cross this way.

A pulse: a toggle

A pulse can come and go between two edges of the reading clock. So each pulse flips a level, the level crosses through a synchroniser, and the reading side turns each change back into a pulse of its own clock: a change either way, up or down, unlike lesson 4.3's edge detector, which sees only rises and would miss every second pulse.

A value: a handshake

The sender holds the value still and raises req. The receiver, seeing req through its synchroniser, takes all the bits at once and raises ack; then both go back down. Every value waits for two crossings each way: in hs_cross_tb, with clocks of 10 ns and about 33 ns, each value takes about 200 ns.

A stream: an asynchronous FIFO

  • A memory written on one clock and read on the other. Each side shows its count to the other in Gray code, where the next value differs in one bit only, so a synchroniser sees the old count or the new one, never a mixture.
  • Full and empty come from the other side's count a few edges late, so they may stay on a little longer than is true, never a little less.

A reset: a reset synchroniser

The reset sets both flip-flops at once, through their asynchronous preset, so it arrives even with no clock running. When it ends, they take 0 one after the other at clock edges, so everything it resets leaves reset at the same edge.

Telling Vivado

Two clocks made from one oscillator, such as the board's and an MMCM's, keep a fixed relationship, and Vivado times every path between them against their closest pair of edges, which a synchroniser's input fails. Clock groups tell it to treat the clocks as unrelated and leave those paths untimed. Its reports then show both: the clock interaction report treats the pair as asynchronous groups, and the CDC report finds the synchroniser. A FIFO's Gray counts also need set_bus_skew, so that the bits of each count arrive within a clock period of each other (UG903).
